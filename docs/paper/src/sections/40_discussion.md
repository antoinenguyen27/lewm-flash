# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Answers to the research questions

**RQ1. How much of the reference planner's latency is redundant work that an exact change removes?** About 40%. Encoding the observation and goal once per plan and keeping the loop on the GPU takes the reference from 622 to 377 ms at batch 1, with identical costs on fixed candidates and identical success to within two episodes. This is the cheapest gain of the ladder and the one any deployment of a JEPA planner should take first, because the released planner treats the encoder as part of the cost function and pays for it in every optimiser iteration.

**RQ2. Does iCEM make planning faster, and does it change task success?** It depends on the execution model, and it does not change success. In eager PyTorch iCEM is 8% slower than CEM although it evaluates 3.19 times fewer rollouts, because each cost call is launch-bound and costs the same at 60 candidates as at 300. In the megakernel it is 1.38 times faster than CEM on the same kernel. In both, its success on 300 paired episodes is within a point of CEM's (265 against 263 eager; 262 against 261 in the kernel). A sample-efficient planner is only as fast as the cost of its smallest batch, and the ladder's ordering matters for its accounting: had iCEM been measured only in eager PyTorch it would have been rejected, and had it been measured only on top of the megakernel its contribution would have been read as a third of what the evaluation count suggests.

**RQ3. How much does executing the whole planner as one persistent kernel gain over the best PyTorch implementation of the same planner?** A factor of 3.1 end to end (182 to 59 ms), and 2.0 on the solve alone (82.3 to 41.3 ms), at the same numerics class and the same number of rollouts. Against the eager implementation of the same planner it is 6.9 times. The megakernel gains by removing the latency between arithmetic, not the arithmetic: host round trips, launches and graph replays, activation traffic through global memory, and per-operator weight reloads.

**RQ4. Do the numeric changes of the megakernel and the fused encoder preserve the planner's accuracy?** Yes, within the resolution of the evaluation. The kernel's cost error against FP32 is the same as that of PyTorch's BF16 autocast, the fused encoder is closer to FP32 than BF16 autocast is, and on 300 paired episodes the final rung differs from the reference by −0.3 points (95% CI −4.3 to +3.7; 19 against 20 discordant). We do not claim the planner is unchanged to better than about 6 points of paired difference, which is what 300 episodes can resolve; we claim that no step of the ladder produced a detectable change, and that the variation between rungs is of the size that a new random stream alone produces.

## 4.2 Why a JEPA planner is a latency problem, not a throughput problem

The workload of a JEPA planner is unusual among neural-network inference workloads, and the ladder's results follow from its shape. The model is tiny and the computation is deep. One plan is 30 optimiser iterations, each a 5-step autoregressive rollout through 6 transformer layers, for 60 to 300 candidates that together form matrices of 64 to 300 rows. The arithmetic of a whole plan is a few hundred GFLOP, which an H100's tensor cores finish in well under a millisecond, and the weights fit in L2. Neither of the two quantities by which inference is usually optimised, FLOPs and bytes moved, is binding. What binds is the length of the dependent chain, 900 layer evaluations per plan, and the fixed cost of each link.

Each rung of the ladder attacks a different term of that fixed cost. Caching removes work from the chain. iCEM shortens each link's width but not the chain's length, which is why its gain is bounded by how much a link's cost depends on its width. Compilation and CUDA graphs, in LeWM-Flash, shorten the per-operator cost of each link but keep the per-iteration host round trip and the global-memory traffic between operators. The megakernel removes both: a link is now a WGMMA chain on data already in shared memory, followed by a cluster barrier. What remains is the hardware's own latency for small dependent operations, measured in Section 3.8 in fractions of a microsecond per barrier and epilogue.

This has a practical consequence for how JEPA world models are deployed. The usual levers of inference optimisation, lower precision and larger batches, help little at batch 1: BF16 roughly halves the arithmetic that was not binding, and batching amortises costs a single robot cannot share. The lever that matters is fusion across the whole planner, and the planner's structure makes that fusion natural: its loop has a static shape once the population schedule is fixed, so the weights it needs, the barriers it crosses and the memory it touches are all known before it starts. That static shape is what allows a weight stream with no producer warp and a schedule with no host involvement.

## 4.3 What the megakernel design needed

Three decisions carried most of the gain, and each has a counterpart in the megakernel literature that we can now confirm for this workload class.

**Weights as a precomputed stream rather than a cache.** Because the planner's sequence of operations is fixed, the panel sequence is known in advance, and each warpgroup can prefetch its next weights the moment it releases a buffer, through any boundary. The weight ring almost never waited. The design choice that made this possible without a producer warp was not an algorithm but a register budget: Hopper's register file gives 255 registers per thread to eight warps and 168 to nine, and every attempt that raised register pressure (peeling, prefetching K and V, prefetching row-owner operands) made the kernel slower through spills.

**Cheap cluster synchronisation.** Tensor parallelism across a cluster puts four cluster barriers in every layer, so the barrier is on the critical path 3,600 times per plan. A barrier with a release on every remote arrive cost 3.6 µs; one fence and relaxed arrives cost 0.65 µs. That single change was worth about 11 ms of the solve (55.3 to 43.8 ms, together with the fast erf). The distributed shared memory of thread-block clusters is what makes tensor parallelism inside a kernel viable at all, but its synchronisation primitives have to be used with the memory ordering they need and no more.

**Numerics chosen to match an accepted path, not to minimise error.** The kernel uses BF16 operands with FP32 accumulation, residual, LayerNorm statistics and costs, because that is the precision class whose closed-loop accuracy the LeWM-Flash study had already accepted. Matching an accepted class let numerical acceptance be a comparison of error distributions on identical inputs rather than a new closed-loop question for every kernel change. The same choice explains why the fused encoder could be adopted quickly: its error is below that of the BF16 autocast path it replaces.

Two properties distinguish the ladder. Among the entries that apply to a neural world model, it is the only one that keeps the model, its weights and the planner fixed, down to the population schedule, elite count and horizon, and still reaches the time scale of control; the other routes buy their gains with retraining, a different optimiser or a learned amortisation, and they compose with ours rather than compete with it. And it is the only one whose fused unit is an entire optimisation loop rather than a single forward pass: the megakernel for LLM decoding fuses one model evaluation per launch, while a planning call is 2,820 evaluations, 30 synchronising reductions and their distribution updates. Against that, it is specific to one model architecture and one GPU generation, and its engineering cost is not comparable to switching on a compiler.

## 4.5 Limitations and threats to validity

**Statistical resolution of the accuracy claim.** Three hundred paired episodes resolve paired differences of about 6 points at conventional power. Every claim that a rung preserves task success is a claim of no detectable change at that resolution. The rungs that change the random stream differ from the reference in 32 to 39 episodes each, so a systematic difference of 2 or 3 points in either direction would not be visible; resolving one would need about 2,400 pairs.

**Latency sample and variance.** Each rung's latency rests on 72 to 76 plans from one process per seed, on one GPU of one cloud provider, measured in two passes. The PyTorch rungs moved by up to 18% between the passes, so their medians are best read as typical rather than exact, and the step gains involving them (1.65× for caching, 0.92× for iCEM in eager mode, 3.1× for the megakernel over LeWM-Flash) carry that uncertainty. The megakernel rungs reproduced to within about 2%. The R2b distribution is bimodal and R4 has one 458 ms outlier; we report both and did not isolate either cause.

**Micro-benchmark and harness disagree in absolute terms.** The isolated eager FP32 solve (601 ms) is slower than the eager rungs' end-to-end plans in the harness (377 to 410 ms), and the composition of Figure 6 combines isolated measurements with an end-to-end median by difference. The conclusions drawn from the micro-benchmarks depend on their shape (flat latency in the population for eager execution; the ordering of the three paths), not on their absolute level, but the decomposition of R3 and R4 is an attribution rather than a measurement.

**One model, one task, one GPU.** All results are for LeWM's Push-T checkpoint on an H100. The kernel's tile shapes, cluster sizes and panel layout are written for this architecture's dimensions (width 192, 16 heads of 64, MLP 2,048, history 3) and for Hopper's WGMMA, clusters and DSMEM; a different JEPA, a longer horizon or a Blackwell GPU would need retuning, and the kernel does not run on the L40S on which LeWM-Flash was developed. The mechanism, a latency-bound chain that fusion shortens, is a property of sampling-based planning with small world models, and we expect the ordering of the ladder to transfer; the factors are specific to this system.

**Evaluation batches and reused runs.** The quality runs were made in three batches over two days, and the reference and LeWM-Flash runs come from the megakernel phase with the ladder's flags rather than from the ladder's own invocation. Episodes are fixed by seed, so the pairing is exact, but the runs were not interleaved, and container-level differences between days cannot be excluded for the PyTorch rungs' latency. They do not affect success, which does not depend on timing.

**Simulation only, and only the planner.** Push-T in simulation is the benchmark on which LeWM reports its planning results, and a 46.5 ms plan is a claim about compute, not about control. A real robot adds sensing and actuation latency, and the control rate it needs depends on the task. We also optimised only the planning call; the 48 non-planning calls of an episode are measured but were optimised only incidentally, by the fused path's removal of the per-call image transform.

**Budget.** The programme ran on rented GPU time against a fixed budget and used about $31.50 of the $35 approved (Appendix D). That bounded the ladder to two latency passes, one quality evaluation per rung and no repeated quality seeds, and it is why the latency of the batched evaluation regime is reported only as an appendix.
