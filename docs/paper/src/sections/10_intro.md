# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background

World models that plan in a learned latent space have become one of the main routes to goal-conditioned robot control without rewards. The joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) is the most compact form of the idea. Instead of reconstructing pixels, a JEPA learns an encoder and a predictor such that the predicted embedding of a future observation matches the encoder's embedding of it (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024). Conditioned on actions, the predictor becomes a dynamics model, and a goal image becomes a target in embedding space: planning is the search for the action sequence whose predicted final embedding lies closest to the goal's. DINO-WM showed that such a model, built on frozen DINOv2 patch features, plans zero-shot on manipulation and navigation tasks (Zhou et al., 2025; Oquab et al., 2024); PLDM made the case for planning with latent dynamics learned from reward-free data (Sobal et al., 2025); and V-JEPA 2-AC carried the approach to real Franka arms from video pre-training (Assran et al., 2025). LeWorldModel (LeWM) is the most recent and the smallest of the line: a 15-million-parameter JEPA trained end to end from pixels with a single Gaussian regulariser, SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025), that reaches 96% success on Push-T with a cross-entropy-method planner (Maes, Le Lidec, Scieur, et al., 2026).

Nearly all of these systems plan the same way. A sampling-based optimiser, the cross-entropy method (CEM; Rubinstein, 1999) or a relative, draws hundreds of candidate action sequences, rolls each through the predictor, keeps the best, refits its sampling distribution to them and repeats, and the robot executes the first actions of the result before planning again (Chua et al., 2018; Hafner et al., 2019). The cost of a plan is therefore the cost of a model evaluation multiplied by the number of candidates and iterations, and for the large world models of the line it has been the obstacle to deployment. DINO-WM takes 53 seconds for one CEM call on an A6000 (Zhou et al., 2025); V-JEPA 2-AC takes 16 seconds per action on an RTX 4090 (Assran et al., 2025); a diffusion-based navigation world model takes 30 seconds to simulate one trajectory (Bar et al., 2025). LeWM was designed against exactly this cost. By encoding each image to a single 192-dimensional token rather than hundreds of patch features, it plans up to 48 times faster than foundation-model world models and completes a plan "in under one second" (Maes, Le Lidec, Scieur, et al., 2026).

Under one second is fast for a world model and slow for a controller. A planner that replans every few control steps must return within the period it plans for; a manipulator closing a contact-rich loop at ten hertz has a budget of 100 milliseconds for sensing, planning and actuation together. On an H100, the released LeWM planner takes 622 ms to produce one plan for one robot. The reasons are not what they are for the larger world models. LeWM's model is so small that a cost evaluation of 300 candidates is a fraction of a millisecond of tensor-core arithmetic, and its weights fit in the GPU's cache. Its plan is slow because the planner, as released, recomputes work it could reuse, issues every operator of every rollout from Python, returns to the CPU in every iteration, and walks a chain of 900 dependent transformer-layer evaluations per plan through a framework built for large batches. The latency of a LeWM plan is a systems problem, and the parts of it that are not are the planner's choice of how many candidates to evaluate.

This paper is about removing that latency without changing what the planner computes. We hold the model, its weights and the semantics of its planner fixed, and we change the inference stack one measured step at a time on one H100: first the exact changes that remove redundant work, then the improved cross-entropy method (iCEM; Pinneri et al., 2021), which evaluates three times fewer candidates, and finally a single persistent GPU kernel, a megakernel, that runs the whole planner from the raw camera frame to the action distribution in one launch. At every step we measure what a deployed controller would see, the end-to-end latency of one replanning call for one robot, and what the task would see, closed-loop success on the same 300 Push-T episodes.

## 1.2 Research significance

The significance of the work is threefold. Scientifically, the planning latency of JEPA world models has been reported only as a by-product of model design: each paper that made planning faster did so by changing the model or the planner (Section 1.4), and none held both fixed and asked how much of the remaining latency is the execution of the planner rather than its mathematics. A measured answer for the smallest model of the line tells the field where the floor is for this class of planner, and which of the familiar levers (fewer samples, lower precision, compilation, fusion) actually move it.

Methodologically, the paper separates three kinds of change that are usually reported together, exact, planner-level and numeric, and measures each on the same hardware against the same reference, with paired closed-loop evaluation for every rung. That separation is what reveals that a planner-level change can be worth nothing in one execution model and a third of its nominal value in another, which a ladder measured in a single execution model would miss.

Practically, a 46.5-millisecond plan for one robot on one GPU, with unchanged task success, moves JEPA planning from the time scale of deliberation to the time scale of control. It does so for the released model and planner, with no retraining and no change to the planner's hyperparameters, so the gain is available to any system that uses LeWM as released, and the design principles behind it, a static weight stream, cluster-level tensor parallelism and an optimiser loop that never leaves the GPU, apply to sampling-based planning with any small world model.

## 1.3 Research questions

The primary question is how far the batch-1 planning latency of a JEPA world model can be reduced on a datacentre GPU without changing the model, the planner's semantics or its task success, and which kinds of change account for the reduction. We decompose it into four questions.

- **RQ1.** How much of the reference planner's latency is redundant work that an exact change removes?
- **RQ2.** Does the improved cross-entropy method make planning faster, by how much, and does it change task success?
- **RQ3.** How much does executing the whole planner as one persistent kernel gain over the best PyTorch implementation of the same planner?
- **RQ4.** Do the numeric changes of the megakernel and of a fused encoder preserve the planner's accuracy, measured against FP32 on identical inputs and in closed loop on paired episodes?

The hypotheses at the start of the H100 programme were that caching would be exact and worth a large share of the reference latency, that iCEM's gain would scale with its 3.19-fold reduction in model evaluations, that a megakernel would beat the compiled BF16 PyTorch path by at least a factor of two, and that none of the steps would change task success. Sections 3 and 4 report that the first, third and fourth held, and that the second was false in eager PyTorch and held at less than half its nominal size in the megakernel.

## 1.4 Literature review

### 1.4.1 JEPA world models and planning in latent space

The joint-embedding predictive architecture was introduced for self-supervised image representation learning: I-JEPA predicts the representations of masked target blocks from a context block rather than reconstructing their pixels (Assran et al., 2023), and V-JEPA extends feature prediction to video (Bardes et al., 2024). The step from representation learning to control was taken by conditioning the predictor on actions. DINO-WM trains a predictor over frozen DINOv2 patch features and plans with CEM-based model-predictive control by minimising latent distance to a goal, reaching 90% success on Push-T (Zhou et al., 2025; Oquab et al., 2024). PLDM plans with a JEPA-style latent dynamics model trained on reward-free offline data and generalises better than model-free goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (Sobal et al., 2025). V-JEPA 2 pre-trains on over a million hours of video, post-trains an action-conditioned predictor on 62 hours of robot data, and plans image-goal manipulation on real arms with CEM at 800 samples and 10 iterations (Assran et al., 2025). A systematic study of what drives planning success in JEPA world models found the CEM family the best planner on Push-T and gradient-based planners poor there, and reported high sensitivity to the planner's population settings, but no planning times (Terver et al., 2026).

LeWM is the model we study (Maes, Le Lidec, Scieur, et al., 2026). It trains a ViT-tiny encoder (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) and a 6-layer transformer predictor with AdaLN action conditioning (Peebles & Xie, 2023) end to end from pixels, with a prediction loss and the SIGReg regulariser, which pushes embeddings towards an isotropic Gaussian and replaces the stop-gradient and teacher-student heuristics of earlier JEPAs (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025). It plans with CEM at 300 samples, 30 elites and 30 iterations on Push-T, horizon 5 with a frame-skip of 5, and its evaluation and reference planners are those of the stable-worldmodel platform (Maes, Le Lidec, Facury, et al., 2026). Its descendants make planning cheaper by changing the representation or the model: Causal-JEPA uses object-level latents with about 1% of the input features of patch-based models (Nam et al., 2026), temporal straightening regularises latent trajectories so that gradient-based planning becomes stable (Y. Wang et al., 2026), and representation geometry has been shown to matter for planning with both CEM and iCEM (Hu et al., 2026). The JEPA planning literature thus treats latency as a property of the model. Where it is reported it is coarse and secondary: 53 s per DINO-WM CEM call, 16 s per V-JEPA 2-AC action, about 1.2 s per LeWM plan against about 92 s per DINO-WM plan in an independent measurement (Hu et al., 2026), and "under one second" for LeWM in its own paper.

### 1.4.2 Sampling-based trajectory optimisation: from CEM to iCEM

The cross-entropy method was introduced for rare-event estimation and combinatorial optimisation and adapted to continuous optimisation by sampling from a parametric distribution, selecting elites and refitting the distribution to them (Rubinstein, 1999; de Boer et al., 2005). PETS established CEM over learned probabilistic dynamics as a standard for model-based reinforcement learning (Chua et al., 2018), and PlaNet made CEM planning in a learned latent space from pixels the template for latent model-predictive control (Hafner et al., 2019). TD-MPC and TD-MPC2 plan in the latent space of a decoder-free world model with a terminal value function and the related MPPI optimiser (Hansen et al., 2022, 2024); model-predictive path integral control was itself designed from the outset as a GPU-parallel sampling method (G. Williams, Aldrich, & Theodorou, 2017) and run in real time through neural-network dynamics (G. Williams, Wagener, et al., 2017).

The improved cross-entropy method, iCEM, is the sample-efficient variant we adopt (Pinneri et al., 2021). It adds four features to CEM: temporally correlated, coloured-noise sampling; keeping a fraction of the elites from one iteration to the next and shifting them in time across replans; executing the best action rather than the mean; and a population that decays exponentially across iterations, N~i~ = max(N γ^−i^, 2K). Together they reduced the samples needed by 2.7 to 22 times on its benchmarks and made real-time planning possible with PlaNet's models in PyTorch. iCEM has been run on LeWM as one of several solvers in an evaluation of representation geometry, without a decay schedule or elite retention and not to reduce latency (Hu et al., 2026), and it is among the solvers stable-worldmodel provides. The alternatives that cut samples by adding gradients, CEM interleaved with gradient steps on the action sequences (Bharadhwaj et al., 2020) and CEM-GD (Huang et al., 2021), illustrate a point central to this paper: CEM-GD uses about a hundred times fewer samples per step than a 5,000-sample CEM and is only 25% faster than it, and it remains several times slower than CEM with 50 to 500 samples, because the per-iteration cost of a planner does not scale with its sample count once the sample count is small. Gradient-based planning with world models has been studied directly and found competitive in sample-efficient settings (S V et al., 2023), but CEM remains the best planner on Push-T for LeWM and for JEPA world models generally (Maes, Le Lidec, Scieur, et al., 2026; Terver et al., 2026).

### 1.4.3 Making world-model planning cheaper

Three routes to cheaper planning have been taken, and each changes something the ladder holds fixed. The first changes the model. Fast LeWorldModel retrains LeWM to predict all action prefixes of a candidate in one parallel pass instead of five autoregressive steps, which halves CEM time on an RTX 4090 (33.7 to 16.1 s over its evaluation workload) at the cost of retraining (Gao & Xu, 2026). Distillation of a diffusion navigation world model cuts its trajectory simulation from 30 to 0.4 s with heavy approximation (Bar et al., 2025). The second changes the planner to use gradients (Bharadhwaj et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2021; S V et al., 2023; Y. Wang et al., 2026), which on Push-T has so far cost success. The third amortises planning into a policy: POPLIN initialises or replaces planning with a policy network (T. Wang & Ba, 2020), and a goal-conditioned inverse-dynamics model trained over a pretrained LeWM matches or beats CEM in 7 of 8 settings at 100 to 130 times lower cost per decision (Nguyen et al., 2026). Each route is complementary to ours, and each gives up something, the released weights, the planner's success or the explicit search, that we keep.

In robotics outside world models, GPU-parallel sampling MPC is mature and fast. STORM computes MPPI-style joint-space control for a Franka arm in under 8 ms (Bhardwaj et al., 2022), cuRobo solves collision-free motion generation in about 50 ms through custom GPU kernels (Sundaralingam et al., 2023), and MPPI-Generic offers a combined CUDA kernel that fuses the dynamics and cost of a rollout with intermediates in shared memory (Vlahov et al., 2024). These systems reach millisecond planning with analytic or small hand-written dynamics, and the lesson they teach, that planning reaches the time scale of control only through custom kernels, has not been applied to a transformer world model with an image encoder.

### 1.4.4 Small-batch inference: launch overhead, data movement and fusion

The roofline model bounds a kernel's throughput by its arithmetic intensity against the machine's memory bandwidth (S. Williams et al., 2009), and the analysis of transformer inference at small batch has shown it to be dominated by memory traffic and latency rather than arithmetic (Pope et al., 2023; Ivanov et al., 2021). A JEPA planner at batch 1 sits below both roofs: its kernels are too small to saturate either the tensor cores or the memory system, and its time goes to launching them and to waiting between them. CUDA Graphs reduce the CPU cost of a launch, from 9.6 µs per kernel with a synchronisation after each launch to 3.4 µs on a V100 in NVIDIA's own measurement, but they do not remove the gaps between kernels (Gray, 2019; NVIDIA, 2026). PyTorch 2's compiler captures graphs and generates fused Triton kernels (Ansel et al., 2024; Tillet et al., 2019), and it is the stack of the strongest PyTorch rung of our ladder; its unit of fusion remains a region of operators inside one model call, with the optimiser loop left on the host.

Fusion inside an operator is the other half of the lineage. FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles held in on-chip memory without materialising the score matrix (Dao et al., 2022), FlashAttention-2 improved its work partitioning across warps (Dao, 2024), and FlashAttention-3 used Hopper's asynchronous tensor-core instructions (WGMMA), its Tensor Memory Accelerator and warp specialisation to reach 85% utilisation in BF16 (Shah et al., 2024). Reduced precision with FP32 accumulation is the standard numerics of this work (Micikevicius et al., 2018), and BF16 keeps FP32's dynamic range without changes to hyperparameters (Kalamkar et al., 2019); our megakernel adopts the same class.

### 1.4.5 Persistent kernels and megakernels

A persistent kernel launches only as many thread blocks as the GPU can hold and loops over work inside them (Gupta et al., 2012). Persistent RNNs used the idea to keep a recurrent network's weights on chip across timesteps at small batch (Diamos et al., 2016), the closest neural-network precedent for a planner whose small predictor is applied 150 times per plan. The idea returned for language-model decoding in 2025 as the megakernel: a whole forward pass in one launch. Hazy Research fused the batch-1 decode of Llama-3.2-1B into one ThunderKittens kernel with an on-GPU instruction interpreter and paged shared memory, removing the "bubbles" between kernels and reaching forward passes under a millisecond on an H100 (Spector, Juravsky, Sul, Dugan, et al., 2025), and then extended it to a tensor-parallel Llama-70B on eight GPUs (Spector, Juravsky, Sul, Lim, et al., 2025). FlashFormer fuses the whole transformer forward pass for low-batch inference (Nrusimha et al., 2025). On the compiler side, Mirage searches for fused kernels automatically (Wu et al., 2025), MPK compiles model inference into one persistent megakernel with in-kernel task scheduling and reports up to 1.7 times lower latency (Cheng et al., 2026), and Event Tensor addresses the shape- and data-dependent dynamism that megakernels must support (Jin et al., 2026).

ThunderKittens is the framework in which our kernel is written: it exposes Hopper's WGMMA and bulk asynchronous copies through warp- and warpgroup-level tiles, and its kernels match cuBLAS on GEMM and FlashAttention-3 on attention (Spector, Arora, et al., 2025). The Hopper features the kernel depends on are documented by NVIDIA, thread-block clusters of up to 16 blocks with distributed shared memory, asynchronous transaction barriers and bulk copies on a 132-SM H100 (Andersch et al., 2022; NVIDIA, 2022), and characterised independently by micro-benchmarks (Luo et al., 2024).

## 1.5 Research gap and contributions

Against this literature the gap is precise. Planning with JEPA world models is CEM-family sampling at its core, and its latency has been reduced only by changing the model, the planner or both; no work has held the model and the planner's semantics fixed and measured what the execution of the planner costs. iCEM has been applied to LeWM as an evaluated solver, but its value for latency, which depends on the execution model beneath it, has not been measured. Megakernels have been built for language-model decoding, where the unit of fusion is one forward pass, and GPU sampling MPC has fused single rollouts with analytic dynamics; no work fuses an entire sampling-based planning loop, with a neural encoder, a multi-step transformer rollout, cost, elite selection and distribution updates across every iteration, into one kernel. The paper makes five contributions.

1. A measured optimisation ladder for JEPA world-model planning on one H100, in which every rung adds one change to the reference LeWM planner and is evaluated for batch-1 end-to-end latency and for paired closed-loop success, separating exact, planner-level and numeric changes.
2. The finding that iCEM's latency value depends on the execution model: it is 8% slower than CEM in eager PyTorch, where cost calls are launch-bound, and 1.38 times faster inside a megakernel, with task success unchanged in both.
3. LeWM-MK, to our knowledge the first megakernel for world-model planning: one persistent ThunderKittens kernel that runs the ViT encoder, the action encoder, the AdaLN predictor rollout with an exact KV cache, the cost and all 30 iCEM iterations with in-kernel sampling and elite selection, using cluster-level tensor parallelism over distributed shared memory and a static weight stream with no producer warp.
4. A batch-1 plan of 46.5 ms against 622 ms for the reference (13.4 times) and 182 ms for the best PyTorch implementation (3.9 times), with p90 within 0.6 ms of the median, and no detectable change in task success on 300 paired episodes (−0.3 points, 95% CI −4.3 to +3.7).
5. A reproducible protocol with committed per-call latency records, per-episode outcomes, numerical parity records and analysis scripts, and an account of the budget it consumed.
