# 5. Future work

The results point to five lines of work, in the order we would pursue them.

**Spend the saved latency on planning.** The ladder held the planner's budget fixed in order to measure its execution, but a deployed planner would choose its budget against its deadline. At 46.5 ms a plan with twice the iterations or the full 300-candidate population in every iteration still fits a 100 ms control period, and the megakernel prices those choices exactly: R3-CEM shows that the full CEM budget costs 23 ms more than iCEM in the kernel. The LeWM-Flash telemetry showed iCEM's elite cost still falling between iterations 25 and 30. A sweep of iterations, population floor and retained elites on the 300 paired episodes, now affordable at a few milliseconds per plan, would map the latency-success frontier of the planner rather than one point on it, and would test whether the 9 points between our reference's 87% and LeWM's reported 96% are within reach of planning budget alone.

**Fewer hand-offs per layer.** The kernel's remaining 41 ms is the latency of a 900-layer dependent chain with four cluster barriers and two row-owner rounds per layer, and the tuning log shows that further register-level work makes it slower. The next step is structural: an all-reduce that removes the owner round trip, or a pipeline in which each cluster holds one layer's weights resident, in FP8 if necessary, and candidates stream through clusters. Either would trade the tensor-parallel schedule's barriers for point-to-point hand-offs, and the primitive costs measured here (0.65 µs per cluster barrier, 1.6 µs per grid barrier) are enough to estimate the gain before writing it.

**Compose with model-level speedups.** The model and planner were held fixed by design, and the routes that change them are complementary. Fast LeWorldModel's parallel prefix prediction would shorten the chain from five predictor passes per iteration to one (Gao & Xu, 2026), which inside the megakernel would remove most of the dependent depth that sets its latency; an amortised inverse-dynamics policy (Nguyen et al., 2026) could warm-start the sampling distribution and allow fewer iterations. Both would need their own paired evaluation, and the ladder's protocol is ready for them.

**Other world models and other hardware.** LeWM's single-token representation makes it the most favourable case for a megakernel; DINO-WM's patch tokens and V-JEPA 2-AC's larger predictor make the planner's matrices larger and push the workload back towards throughput, where the case for fusing the whole optimiser loop should be measured rather than assumed. On the hardware side, the kernel is written for Hopper; Blackwell's tensor-memory and larger clusters, and onboard computers such as the Jetson Thor, change both the primitive costs and the arithmetic budget. An automated megakernel compiler that supports the shape dynamism of iCEM (Cheng et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026) would make that porting a compilation rather than a rewrite, and LeWM's planner is a compact test case for one.

**Evaluate at the resolution a deployment needs, and on a robot.** Three hundred paired episodes exclude changes of about 6 points in task success and no smaller. A deployment that cares about 2 points needs about 2,400 paired episodes per comparison, which at the megakernel's speed is now an evaluation of minutes rather than hours, and a sequential paired test would let it stop early. The final step is a closed loop on a real robot, where the question becomes not how fast a plan is computed but how fast the planner needs to be for the task, and whether replanning at every control step, which a 46.5 ms plan permits and the released 622 ms plan did not, improves what the robot achieves.

# 6. Conclusion

We set out to measure how far the planning latency of a JEPA world model can be reduced without changing the model, the planner's semantics or its task success, and which kinds of change account for the reduction. We built a seven-rung ladder on one H100 from the released LeWM planner, adding one change per rung, and evaluated every rung for the end-to-end latency of one replanning call for one robot and for closed-loop success on the same 300 Push-T episodes.

The answer has three parts. Exact changes, encoding the observation and goal once per plan and keeping the optimiser on the GPU, remove 40% of the reference latency (622 to 377 ms) at no cost to anything. The improved cross-entropy method, which evaluates 3.19 times fewer candidate rollouts, is worth nothing in eager PyTorch, where every cost call is launch-bound and costs the same at 60 candidates as at 300, and 1.38 times inside a megakernel; its value is a property of the execution model as much as of the algorithm. And executing the whole planner, encoder, predictor rollouts, cost, elite selection and distribution updates for all 30 iterations, as one persistent ThunderKittens kernel takes a plan to 46.5 ms with a p90 of 47.0 ms: 13.4 times faster than the reference and 3.9 times faster than the best PyTorch implementation of the same planner, with BF16 operands and FP32 accumulation whose error matches the accepted autocast path and whose closed-loop success is indistinguishable from the reference (−0.3 points, 95% CI −4.3 to +3.7).

The mechanism is the shape of the workload. A JEPA planner is a small model applied in a long chain of dependent steps, and at batch 1 it is bound neither by arithmetic nor by memory bandwidth but by the latency of each link. The levers that matter are the ones that shorten that chain or the fixed cost of its links, and the one that matters most is to stop leaving the GPU between them.

LeWM made world-model planning fast enough to discuss for control by shrinking the model. This paper shows that the execution of the planner, not its mathematics, was most of what remained, and that removing it takes JEPA planning from the time scale of deliberation to the time scale of control on the hardware where these models are trained.
